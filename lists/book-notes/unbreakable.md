> [...] Plan a period, after a big emotional and physical commitment, in which you can decompress. Work out what you need to be able to go again. Maybe it’s sleep, maybe it’s a change of scene. Maybe it’s limiting your screen time or building in a week where you’re going to get ten hours’ sleep a night. Know what you need to do.

> There’s something Stirling Moss said when he was at the peak of his game in Formula One. Someone asked him: what’s it like driving at that speed? And he said, ‘When I’m driving well, it feels slow. I see everything coming – every corner, every decision. It only feels fast when you’re driving badly. Then the corners come on you too quickly. Then the panic comes. The fear.’

> There was a lesson for me, when I was ready to accept it. Be careful who you let into your circle. There are some dangerous people out there, and they’ll quite happily use you to get what they want.

> I’m naturally a quiet bloke. I observe, I listen. I like order, and peace, and things that work.

> When I gave up playing for a year after winning the Worlds in 2012, my phone went very quiet very fast. It’s as if you become invisible. My true friends were still there, but 99 per cent of the people I used to speak to on a daily basis disappeared overnight. When I decided to come back and play snooker – bang, the phone was ringing again.

> <mark>I’m not fighting the world, I’m trying to navigate my way quietly through it.</mark>

> ‘What’s the matter with you, Ronnie?’\
> ‘I’ve had a shit day.’\
> Then I thought, I can’t sit here too long talking about how shit my day has been. How’s that helping? I’ve got to do something about it. I went through the kitchen and out into the snooker room. I played for about an hour and three-quarters, and I absolutely loved it. I put my cue down and my head was totally clear. Okay, we’re back.

> A friend of mine who knows about these things tells me he thinks I might have autism. The way I play the game. He means it as a compliment, and I think he might be right. It definitely helps in snooker. <mark>You want to bring order to the chaos. You want every ball in a pleasing place.</mark>

> I love all this. Stuff that only a very few of us on the planet can underestand.

> Being the best in sport is about that combination of the scientific and the instinctive. It’s about taking the advice of others but putting your own stamp on it too. Not copying someone just because it works for them. <mark>And it’s about caring, more than anyone else. There will be things in your life that you love. Do them your way, and let that love shine through.</mark>

> Ray Reardon had a great phrase for it. ‘Let it come to you, Ronnie …’ [...] He understood that clinging on to something too tightly will only lead to broken fingernails and broken hearts.

> Keep your head, keep fighting. Be brave. Even if you don’t feel like you’ve got the bollocks, act like you have.

> <mark>Competition can leave you exhausted and broken, but it will also bring out the best in you.</mark>

> You can’t keep it going, because the effort you’re putting in is too great. You can’t play with such intensity in the long term. And if you haven’t figured it out, and found an alternative way to win, it starts to eat away at you. You question everything you’re doing.

> Sometimes I’ve had to make an apology to someone, even when my instinct told me it was all their fault. <mark>But by cleaning your side of the street, you help release that negative energy.</mark> Don’t keep spewing out anger. It never helps.

> [...] to resist the urge to sabotage, to say to myself, <mark>let’s just go with what we’ve got here,</mark> get any sort of win, not worry about the manner of it. To let tomorrow be another day.

> My job is to play snooker. To do that I must have a clear mind. <mark>When I’m scrolling, it’s taking me in the opposite direction.</mark>

> I joined Instagram when a friend told me it would be good for business if I could post stories, and within a week my head was a mess. As soon as I ditched it, I felt significantly better. Twitter? I had my kids for the weekend a little while ago, and I got into an argument with another snooker player on it and was lost for three hours. It felt so wrong.

> <mark>When you sprinkle each day with things you enjoy, the day becomes a good one.</mark> It might be you having a coffee with a friend. It might be taking your dog for a walk or giving yourself half an hour to read a book or make a meal you’ve never made before.

> We have to control the pace, or we will lose the happiness that doing our thing brings.

> Going for a run on a cold morning, getting in and having a cup of tea and a scone? That’s brilliant, to me.

> Remember the dimmer switch. The light is never off. You just play around with the brightness.

> I’ve got my trust in data because I don’t trust myself [...] my emotions get in the way, and when I get too emotional, I’m all over the place. <mark>I have to write things down</mark> and become scientific – a sleep diary, a running diary, a snooker diary [...] It takes no time, it’s easy, and it’s free. What has made you happy that day? What went well, and what would you change?

> There is such a strong lesson for us in [...] the obsession with everything being absolutely flawless. Sometimes you have to ignore your feelings of doubt. Stop asking yourself if you’re doing something right or wrong, and just get on with it. Put one foot in front of the other. Do what you set out to do.

> You’ve learned now, yeah? <mark>One ball at a time,</mark> just pot, pot, keep focused, pot. Stop thinking about speeches. Don’t even make a speech, just win. Just stay in the present.

> All the success in the world is not worth it if you’re not able to enjoy it.

> <mark>The best thing you can ever do for your kids is to help them identify their passion and then follow it into adult life.</mark>

> It’s the same as playing against Mark Williams and John Higgins. As being beaten by Stephen Hendry. They’ve made me want to be better. It’s a good thing for all of us to be around people who do this to us.

> I felt safe, and I hadn’t felt safe in a long time. [...] <mark>I had appreciated what being embedded in a community can do for you.</mark> It’s companionship and support. It’s feeling like you belong. I still felt lonely at snooker tournaments. I couldn’t let my guard down. Everyone looking for flaws and weaknesses. Half of them talking behind my back.

> You won’t find many arseholes in the running community, that’s the thing. It’s a great leveller. If you’ve got an ego and you’re a runner, you’ll be a lonely person.

> They’re sitting there drinking, playing cards and talking about snooker until midnight or one in the morning, and then they’re at the venue talking about rankings and tournaments and politics in snooker. I just don’t want to be around that. I find it boring in comparison to the empty trails and early-morning skies.

> Sometimes, if you’re really busy, <mark>it can just be putting things in your diary to look forward to. That always makes me feel happier.</mark> A trip away in a month’s time, a meal you’re going to cook. The thought of lunch with an old friend might be enough to get you through a bad day. If you fancy something else? <mark>Life is as much about recovery as hard effort.</mark> When you’re under stress, the body and mind won’t allow adaptations to take place. Stay calm and relaxed, let yourself rest. I had a week off before the World Championship in 2012, and look what happened there.

> <mark>Suffering in silence seldom works in the long run.</mark> [...] You don’t have to keep talking about it, but you need to get it off your chest.

> Establishing boundaries has balanced out the natural people-pleaser in me.

> A friend of mine from rehab said to me once: Ronnie, <mark>you’re more like a human doing, rather than a human being. He didn’t mean it in a complimentary way.</mark>

> <em>Feel good, play good. Feel shit, play shit, lose, go home.</em>\
> Right. The challenge to that was a simple one: your game might be shit, but <mark>your mind can be good.</mark>

> <mark>I know the chimp will do his best to tell me I’m not good enough.</mark> He’ll start busy and get louder, and once I’m listening to him, he’ll run away with me – I’m not feeling good, I’m past my best, I want to go home … Fine. That’s just the chimp doing his job, trying to hijack me. So I’m going to do my job. I’ll start with a blank piece of paper, with one question at the top: how would I like to feel here? Then I write down my answers. I want to feel focused. Determined. I want to compete. I want to enjoy it. And I want to win, because winning is nice, and we all like to win, we’re just not going to be defined by it.

> If I had a good day at snooker, I was as high as a kite that night. If I had a bad day, I felt like I hated myself. What way is that to live your life? Two decades on, it’s like Steve Peters reminds me: Ronnie, you have friends who will love you no matter what. Whether you play snooker or do something else.

> What pulled me back from the edge was realising I didn’t want a life looking at somebody else wishing I was them.

> Over your lifetime, just as over a thirty-year career like mine, you are going to have bad years. You will also lose your edge at some point. That’s fine. It’s natural.

> When you understand it’s just a bad day, and that tomorrow may well be completely different, it sets you free. The world is not ending; your emotions are just crowding in on you.

> The bad days will probably still happen to you, as they happen to me too. So will the good. That’s okay, as long as we remind ourselves it’s all natural.

> And you have to enjoy it all, or it means nothing. Rule number one, of all my rules, is to be grateful you’re enjoying playing the game. Rule number two: don’t forget rule number one.
